# A. Career Pathways: NASCAR / Stock Cars, Asphalt Short Tracks, and Dirt Ovals (North America, 2020s)

Research brief for an OOTP-style racing career sim. Facts are sourced (see **Sources**). Figures marked **(approx.)** are estimates or ranges from interviews. Items marked **(unverified)** come from general knowledge and could not be confirmed against a source during this research. Status is as of October 2026.

---

## 1. The Big Picture

North American oval racing has two connected trees. Talent and money pass between them.

- **Asphalt / stock-car tree:** quarter midgets or karting → Bandolero → Legends → limited/street stock or mini stock → Late Model Stock (LMSC) / Pro Late Model (PLM) / Super Late Model (SLM) → regional tours (CARS, ASA STARS and its regionals, ARCA East/West, Whelen Modified) → ARCA Menards Series → NASCAR Craftsman Truck Series → NASCAR O'Reilly Auto Parts Series (named Xfinity Series until 2025) → NASCAR Cup Series.
- **Dirt tree:** quarter midgets / junior sprints / outlaw karts → restricted micro sprints → 600cc micro sprints → midgets (USAC, POWRi, Xtreme) and 360 sprints → 410 sprints (World of Outlaws, High Limit, PA Posse, regional) or dirt late models (crate → super; Lucas Oil, WoO Late Model). A separate weekly-racer tree runs IMCA sport compact → hobby stock → stock car → modified.
- **Bridges:** Manufacturer programs, Toyota above all, use midget racing as a scouting pool for NASCAR (Larson, Bell, Abreu, McIntosh, Crews). Hendrick signed Corey Day straight out of California 410 sprint cars. In the other direction, NASCAR veterans often go back down to dirt or late models (Schrader, Wallace, Labonte, Kenseth, Harvick).

Key structural facts for the sim:
1. **Money gates advancement more than talent does**, from late models through Xfinity/O'Reilly. Above that, performance matters more because Cup seats and manufacturer contracts are performance-scouted. Even at that level, pay-drivers fill much of the back of the field.
2. **Age gates are hard rules** (see §3). Elite prospects therefore reach ARCA at 15, Trucks at 16, O'Reilly at 17 (since 2026) and Cup at 18. Late bloomers also exist: Josh Berry was a Cup rookie at 33 and Ty Majeski a Truck champion at 30.
3. **Dirt pays on performance** (purse share). **Stock cars pay on contracts and sponsorship.** These are two different economic models.

## 3. Licensing, Age Rules and Approval

### NASCAR national series minimum ages (2026 rules)
| Series | Short ovals (≤1.25 mi) & road courses | Larger ovals / superspeedways |
|---|---|---|
| ARCA Menards (incl. East/West) | **15** (tracks <1 mi and road courses) | **17** on speedways; **18** at Daytona/Talladega |
| Craftsman Truck | **16** | **18** |
| O'Reilly Auto Parts (ex-Xfinity) | **17** (new for 2026; was 18 everywhere) | **18** |
| Cup | **18** at all tracks | **18** |

NASCAR announced the 2026 changes on 1 Nov 2025 at Phoenix. In the same announcement, Cup drivers with 3+ full-time Cup seasons may now enter up to **10 O'Reilly** races (was 5) and **8 Truck** races (was 5). They remain barred from the regular-season finale and all playoff races. These limits had been in place since 2020.

### Approval process ("license by track type")
- **ARCA:** a driver submits a *Driver Information and Record* résumé to the **ARCA Resume Committee**. The committee decides whether to approve the driver for ARCA national and/or East/West, and **which track types and sizes** are allowed (e.g., "tracks ≤1 mile" or "designated road courses"). A **superspeedway license** requires a qualifying lap speed during Daytona testing. Critics point out that some drivers were approved after only **2–3 late model or dirt starts**. The 2025 Daytona opener had cautions covering 41% of the race distance and only 18 of 40 finishers. One commentator proposed requiring 20+ prior starts (30 for under-18s).
- **NASCAR Cup:** approvals are run by **Chad Little** (succeeding Brett Bodine), with input from Elton Sawyer. Approval is granted **by track category** (e.g., "ovals ≤1 mile + road courses", "≤1.5 miles"). Road-course approval needs a **test day with a NASCAR observer** (Shane van Gisbergen tested at the Charlotte Roval before winning on his 2023 Chicago debut). Examples:
  - Katherine Legge, 44, was approved for road courses and ovals ≤1 mile.
  - Helio Castroneves was approved for the Daytona 500 after testing, sim work and ARCA starts.
  - Mike Wallace was **denied** for Daytona because he had been inactive too long.
  - Casey Mears was approved for ovals ≤1.5 mi.
  - Kyle Busch called the process "broken". Hamlin and Suárez want tougher standards.
- **USAC:** general license at **15+** (a birth certificate is required for ages 15–18). National Midget requires **16+**.
- **Weekly/local tracks:** each track sets its own age limits. This patchwork is why families relocate, as the Gordons did from Vallejo, CA to Pittsboro, IN.

## 4. The Asphalt Ladder Above Local Racing

### Regional touring series (2025–26)
- **CARS Tour** (Southeast): has a **Late Model Stock Tour** and a **Pro Late Model Tour**. Since January 2023 it has been owned by **Dale Earnhardt Jr., Jeff Burton, Kevin Harvick and Justin Marks**, and **Speedway Motorsports** later joined the group. 2025 champions were Landen Lewis (LMSC) and Ben Maier (PLM). Many NASCAR prospects run CARS during their late-model years.
- **CARS Tour West / SRL Southwest** (California/Arizona, largely at Harvick's Kern Raceway in Bakersfield): Super Late, Pro Late, Limited Pro Late and Legends divisions. 2025 champions included Jace Hansen (SLM).
- **ASA STARS National Tour** (Bob Sargent / Track Enterprises): a national Super Late Model series built from three races each from the **ASA/CRA Super Series** (Indiana/Ohio/Michigan), the **ASA Midwest Tour** (Wisconsin; named ARCA Midwest Tour 2012–2022) and the **ASA Southern Super Series**. The 2025 schedule covered 12 tracks in 8 states, from the Clyde Hart Memorial at New Smyrna to the All American 400 at Nashville. Points fund is **$100,000+**, with at least $25k to the champion owner.
- **ARCA Menards East (8 races in 2025) and West (12 races)** are NASCAR-owned. NASCAR bought ARCA in April 2018, and K&N East/West were rebranded under ARCA in 2020. Several races run as combination events with the national ARCA series or as companion events to NASCAR weekends (Phoenix, Sonoma, Portland, LVMS Bullring).
- **NASCAR Whelen Modified Tour** (Northeast): 16 races in 2025. 2025 champion Austin Beers; 2024 champion Justin Bonsignore. The **SMART Modified Tour** is its Southeast counterpart.
- **NASCAR Advance Auto Parts Weekly Series** connects local tracks to a national points system. The national championship pays **$25,000** (best 18 finishes). Regional LMSC titles returned (first time since 2004). Connor Hall won the national title twice in a row and was then hired into the JR Motorsports late model program. Earlier national champions include Josh Berry (2020) and Philip Morris (unverified).
- **Kulwicki Driver Development Program**: the only development program that **pays** its drivers. Each driver gets $7,000 in money, goods and services, and finalists compete for $30k. 102 applicants were cut to 15 semifinalists in one cycle, and it has awarded about $1.04M since 2014. Ty Majeski won its inaugural title in 2015.

### ARCA Menards Series (national)
- About **20 races**: superspeedways, intermediates, short tracks, road courses and dirt.
- **Cost:** a Venturini Motorsports Toyota ride costs **about $100,000 per race** (approx.), so a full season is about $2M. Car owner Larry Clement put an ARCA budget at about **10% of a Cup budget**. Costs are held down by spec Five Star bodies, two spec engine programs (Ilmor and Roush Yates) and fixed "race breaks" that eliminate pit-crew competition.
- At the top teams, **Toyota often supplies the driver and the sponsor** through business-to-business partners (JBL, SiriusXM, Mobil 1, Safelite).
- The back of the field is filled by part-timers, many of them under-prepared pay-drivers.
- Graduates include Kyle Busch, Justin Allgaier (2008 champion), Ty Gibbs (2021 champion → Xfinity champion → Cup), Chase Briscoe (2016 champion) and Rajah Caruth. Frank Kimmel won 10 titles and stayed in ARCA, an example of a "career ARCA" driver.

### NASCAR Craftsman Truck Series
- **About 25 races** (2025).
- **Per-race cost of a good ride: about $130,000** (Tommy Baldwin, 2025).
- **Full-season team cost:** $3.2M (Kyle Busch, KBM) to $4.5M (Brad Keselowski, counting reinvested purse and manufacturer support).
- Hailie Deegan: a top Truck ride costs **about $2M/yr** in driver-brought funding.
- Purses are small. The Darlington Truck race paid $839,700 in total against $1,653,590 for the O'Reilly race there. Baldwin called Trucks "a money pit": staff still has to be paid year-round for fewer races.
- The flagship development series for Toyota (TRICON Garage) and Chevrolet (Spire, which bought KBM in Sept 2023).

### NASCAR O'Reilly Auto Parts Series (Xfinity until 2025)
- Per-race cost **$130k–160k** (Baldwin). Deegan put a top ride at **$4–5M/yr**.
- A FanAmp report (as cited by Frontstretch) said drivers are expected to bring **at least $1M** for a full-time ride. A seat expected to finish 25th or worse costs **$40k–45k per race**, and a top team (JRM, JGR) costs **$100k–150k per race**. Some teams add a **$10k–15k "crash clause" deposit**.
- Primary sponsorship sells for $30k–100k per race ($1M–3.5M per season); associate sponsorship sells for $5k–20k per race.
- Rule since 2026: Cup veterans may run up to 10 races.

### NASCAR Cup Series
- **About $20M per car per season** (Denny Hamlin), excluding driver salary and overhead. A Next Gen car costs about $350k. Engine leases run **$0.8M–1.5M per season**.
- Charters sold for **$20–40M** in 2023–24. Antitrust filings showed teams **lost about $2.2M per car** in 2024. Chartered teams earn about **$7–18M per season** from NASCAR before sponsorship.
- Cup seats are rarely bought outright. Funding does still matter for open (non-chartered) entries and for the weakest chartered teams.

## 5. The Dirt Ladder

### Midgets
- **Series:** USAC National Midget, POWRi National/West Midget League (16 tracks and 38 events in one season), and the Xtreme Outlaw Midget Series (WoO).
- **Keith Kunz Motorsports** (Columbus, IN) has been aligned with **Toyota since 2009**. It works as Toyota's scouting and finishing school. Graduates include Larson, Bell, Rico Abreu, Tanner Thorson, Buddy Kofoid, Cannon McIntosh and Jacob Denney. Kunz spotted an 18-year-old Larson at Perris Auto Speedway in 2010.
- **Chili Bowl Nationals** (Tulsa, indoor 1/5-mile, January, since 1987) is effectively a midget-racing combine:
  - Entries: **341–371** (371 in 2025; 40th edition in 2026). Five qualifying nights feed a Saturday A-main with an elimination ladder from P-main to A-main.
  - Pay: the 2024 winner received $20k plus lap money (Logan Seavey earned $30k+). Making the A-main paid $2k.
  - Kyle Larson won in 2025 (his 3rd). Sammy Swindell holds the record with 5.
  - NASCAR, WoO, IndyCar and NHRA drivers enter. A strong run there raises a driver's profile sharply.

### Sprint cars (410 / 360)
- **World of Outlaws Sprint Car Series**: 90-night calendar in 2025 (41 tracks, 21 states, 2 countries).
  - In 2023: a points fund of about $1.6M, plus about $390k in tow money; a standard race purse of about $58k (rising to $70k).
  - Top earner Logan Schuchart made **$1.43M**. Full-time tow money was about $30–35k per team.
- **High Limit Racing**: founded 2022 by **Kyle Larson and Brad Sweet**.
  - Ran 12 midweek events in 2023, then 50+ nights and about **$5M in purses** in 2024.
  - Points fund $1M, with $250k to the champion.
  - Has a **franchise/charter system**: 10 charters, up to 15 planned. Charter teams get 50% of streaming revenue and must enter every race.
  - Champions: Larson (2023), Sweet (2024), Rico Abreu (2025). First FS1 national TV race in 2025.
- **Pennsylvania**: the "Posse" 410 weekly culture at Williams Grove, Port Royal and Lincoln. A **PA Posse 410 Series** of 47 races at 11 tracks starts in 2026, with at least $6k to win and $600 to start. 358 sprints (since 1989 at Williams Grove) act as the step below 410s.
- **Crown jewels:**
  - **Knoxville Nationals** (Iowa, since 1961): winner paid **$195k** in 2025. Won by Ryan Timms at 18, the second-youngest winner. Larson won 2020, 2021, 2023 and 2024.
  - **Kings Royal** (Eldora, now part of High Limit): **$200k** to win; Anthony Macri won in 2025.
  - **USAC 4-Crown Nationals** at Eldora: Larson swept midget, sprint and Silver Crown on one night in 2011.
- **Driver pay in dirt:** a hired driver usually gets **30–40% of winnings** (sometimes 30% base plus 5% for wins). A good season pays $70–110k from team winnings of $200–300k; a bad season pays $25–35k. There is **no salary safety net**.

### Dirt late models
- **Lucas Oil Late Model Dirt Series**: 58 events at 26 tracks in 2026.
- **World of Outlaws CASE Late Models**: 57 races at 32 tracks in 2025. Points fund over $1M, with **$225k** to the champion.
- **Crown jewels (Eldora):**
  - **Dirt Late Model Dream**: $100k to win, about $700k total including preliminaries ($30k-to-win prelims).
  - **World 100**: $100k to win from 2026 and at least $5,600 to start. Bobby Pierce won the 2025 Dirt Track World Championship ($100k).
- **Costs:** a national program rose from $245–260k (2020) to $450k+ (2024), about $3,350 per night just to unload. Top teams spend $750k–1M. Purses have barely moved: Randy Weaver noted $10k-to-win races paid the same in the mid-1990s.
- Dirt late models are mostly a **self-contained career**. Few drivers move from there to NASCAR (Brandon Overton, Jonathan Davenport and others run only occasional NASCAR/Truck dirt or one-off starts) (unverified detail).

**Patterns:**
- Elite prospects in the 2020s go **from first car to Cup in about 4–6 years**: about 2–4 years in late models or dirt, 1–2 in ARCA, 0–2 in Trucks, 1–2 in O'Reilly.
- Typical prospects need about 8–12 years. Many stall at Trucks or O'Reilly at ages 22–27.
- Late bloomers reach the top through dominant short-track results plus a patron: Berry through Dale Jr., Majeski through Ford/ThorSport.

## 9. Pay-Driver Economics and Funding Collapse

- **Below Cup, rides are largely bought.** A driver (through family wealth, the family business or personal sponsors) brings $1M+ for a full O'Reilly or Truck season. Typical prices are $40–45k per race at a back-marker team and $100–150k per race at a front-runner, plus crash-clause deposits.
- **Part-time "race buying"** is common: drivers buy 5–15 races in a team's second or third car. Team owners admit they swap drivers within a season to whoever has funding. In one case, a ThorSport driver's schedule was halved to make room for a funded driver.
- **Champions can lose rides.** Brett Moffitt was released by Hattori Racing after winning the 2018 Truck title because he did not bring enough money.
- **What happens when the money runs out:**
  1. Drop to a part-time schedule, or to a smaller team.
  2. Step down a level (O'Reilly → Trucks → ARCA).
  3. **Return to late models or super late models** and rebuild results. Majeski did this in 2019, winning 3 of 6 ARCA starts.
  4. Move into an industry role. Majeski worked as a Roush engineer before ThorSport hired him to drive. Other ex-drivers become **spotters, driver coaches, crew chiefs or team managers**; former Cup driver Chad Little runs approvals (role pattern unverified as a statistic).
  5. Switch to dirt, where a top driver can still earn a living from the purse share.
- **Manufacturer backing replaces family money** for the best prospects. Toyota brings B2B sponsors to its development drivers, and Hendrick and Trackhouse fund their prospects. This is the main way a less-wealthy talent can reach the top.
- **Diversity programs and Kulwicki** are small funding channels open to non-wealthy drivers.

## 10. Sideways Moves

- **Dirt → NASCAR (classic route):**
  - Jeff Gordon: USAC Midget/Silver Crown → Busch → Hendrick Cup, 1993.
  - Tony Stewart: USAC Triple Crown in 1995 → IRL champion 1997 → JGR Busch 1998 → Cup 1999.
  - Kasey Kahne: Washington sprints → USAC → Busch 2002.
  - Kyle Larson and Christopher Bell: KKM midgets.
  - Corey Day: 410s → Hendrick.
- **Open wheel / sports cars → NASCAR:**
  - Stewart (IRL).
  - Zilisch: karting/IMSA LMP2. He won the Rolex 24 and Sebring LMP2 class in 2024 at 17.
  - Crews: Trans-Am TA2 champion at 15.
  - Castroneves, Legge.
- **Road-course specialists ("ringers")** are hired for road courses only. Examples: Boris Said (100+ starts since 1997) and Ron Fellows (Xfinity wins, 13 road-course-only Cup starts). Shane van Gisbergen took this further: a Supercars champion who won his Cup debut in Chicago in 2023 and then **won 5 of 6 road races in 2025**, all 7 of his Cup wins coming on road or street courses.
- **NASCAR → dirt:**
  - Larson co-founded High Limit and remains a top sprint and midget driver (4 Knoxville Nationals, 3 Chili Bowls).
  - Stewart became a team owner (WoO sprints) and track owner (Eldora).
  - Cup drivers routinely enter the Chili Bowl.

## 11. Veterans Going Back to Regional and Local Racing

- **Ken Schrader** (born 1955): still racing at 70. He ran **70+ dirt and short-track races in 2024**, including limited ARCA starts, and owns a dirt late model and a dirt modified. In 2013 he became the oldest NASCAR pole winner at age 58, at Eldora.
- **Kenny Wallace** (born 1963): left NASCAR for full-time dirt modified and late model racing (UMP/DIRTcar). He has announced a final full-time season and has since moved to a reduced "half-mile" dirt schedule.
- **Bobby Labonte** (about 60): full-time on the **SMART Modified Tour** in 2022–23 (4 wins). Also made Whelen Modified Tour starts for Sadler-Stanley Racing in 2024.
- **Matt Kenseth**: won the Slinger Nationals a record 8 times. He returns to Wisconsin super late models between and after his NASCAR stints and beat Majeski at Slinger in one classic.
- **Johnny Sauter**: a former Truck champion who still enters Wisconsin SLM races such as the Slinger Nationals. The Sauter family includes a third-generation Kulwicki finalist.
- **Kevin Harvick**: retired from Cup after 2023. He races Pro and Super Late Models with his son **Keelan**, who won at Kern Raceway and Hickory in 2025, through a **KHI and Rackley W.A.R. partnership**. He also co-owns the CARS Tour and owns Kern Raceway, and runs **KHI Management**, an agency managing driver careers and sponsorship.
- **Dale Earnhardt Jr.**: still makes occasional LMSC starts (Tri-County, CARS Cordele), co-owns the CARS Tour, and owns JRM, whose late model program is "what the company was founded on".

**Pattern:** a retired Cup driver often becomes a part-time late model or dirt racer, plus an owner or promoter, plus a mentor to young drivers. These veterans are strong but beatable "boss" opponents in regional crown jewels.

## 12. How Owners, Teams, Sponsors, Agents and Families Shape Careers

- **Family-owned driver-development teams** run by stars:
  - **Kyle Busch Motorsports** (2010–2023; sold to Spire in Sept 2023): 100 Truck wins, titles with Erik Jones (2015) and Bell (2017). First national-series starts for Bell, Jones and Byron. Also fielded trucks for Wallace, Suárez, Harrison Burton and Todd Gilliland.
  - **JR Motorsports**: late model through Xfinity/O'Reilly. Developed Josh Berry, Byron, Kvapil, Zilisch and Connor Hall.
  - **Kevin Harvick Inc.**: Truck and Xfinity titles in the 2000s. Now runs late models with Rackley W.A.R. and KHI Management.
  - Brad Keselowski Racing was Ford's first development stop (Briscoe).
- **Cup owners** sign prospects to **multi-year development deals** before they reach national series: Ganassi signed Larson at 19 in 2012, Trackhouse signed Zilisch in 2024, Hendrick signed Day in 2024. They then **place them with satellite teams** (Spire Cup cars for Day, JRM O'Reilly cars for Zilisch). Charter limits on cars per team create bottlenecks. Heim, the 2025 Truck champion, had **no full-time 2026 Cup seat** and ran 12 races before a 2027 full-time deal.
- **Manufacturers** act as scouts, funders (directly or through B2B sponsors) and gatekeepers of Cup seats.
- **Sponsors:** at most levels the driver "owns" the sponsor relationship. Family businesses often act as sponsors (e.g., Rackley Roofing/W.A.R.) (pattern; specific cases unverified).
- **Agents and managers** (e.g., KHI Management) handle contracts, sponsor sourcing and media. They matter most at the Truck to Cup transition.
- **Families** fund everything from age 5 to about 18. A late model season costs $150–200k and a national midget rental about $300k. Families relocate (the Gordons to Indiana) and often buy or field the team themselves (Crews formed his own Truck team in 2025).

## 13. Regional Geography: How a Driver from Each Region Typically Starts

- **Southeast (NC/VA/SC/GA/FL/AL)** is the late model hotbed.
  - Ladder: Bandolero/Legends (Charlotte, Atlanta Motor Speedway) or karting (GoPro Motorplex, Mooresville) → limited late models → LMSC (VA/NC: South Boston, Hickory, Langley, Dominion) or PLM/SLM (Florida, Alabama, Georgia: Five Flags, New Smyrna, Montgomery) → CARS Tour / Southern Super Series → ARCA.
  - Proximity to Charlotte-area teams is the region's biggest advantage. Crown jewels: Martinsville 300, Snowball Derby.
- **Midwest asphalt (Wisconsin, Michigan, Indiana, Ohio).**
  - Wisconsin: karts → Slinger, Dells, LaCrosse, Madison weekly SLM → ASA Midwest Tour (Kenseth, Majeski, Sauters, Fenhaus).
  - Indiana/Ohio/Michigan: ASA/CRA Super Series and the Winchester 400 (Carson Hocevar swept the CRA Triple Crown).
- **Midwest dirt (Indiana, Ohio, Illinois, Missouri).**
  - USAC heartland. Quarter midgets, micros or outlaw karts → USAC midgets and sprints (non-wing). Eldora and Kokomo are central.
  - The Kunz/Toyota pipeline is in Columbus, IN.
- **Pennsylvania.**
  - Central PA 410 sprint culture (Williams Grove, Port Royal, Lincoln; the "Posse"). The ladder runs micros/358s → 410s.
  - Most drivers there stay sprint-car careerists rather than moving to NASCAR.
- **Iowa / Plains.** IMCA weekly ladder: sport compact → hobby stock → stock car → modified. Knoxville 360/410 sprints, with the Knoxville Nationals as the peak.
- **Oklahoma.** Micro sprints and midgets (Tulsa), the Chili Bowl and Christopher Bell's roots.
- **California.**
  - Dirt: outlaw karts (Elk Grove/Lemoore), restricted and open micro sprints → King of the West 410s (Larson, Day, Gordon's early years).
  - Asphalt: Legends/SLM at Kern Raceway (Harvick-owned, Bakersfield) → CARS Tour West / SRL Southwest → ARCA West, which shares NASCAR weekends at Phoenix and Sonoma.
- **New England / Northeast.** SK Lite/SK modifieds (Stafford, Thompson) and Riverhead → Whelen Modified Tour. In Maine, late models peak with the Oxford 250 and PASS super late models. The region has a few NASCAR graduates and many career modified racers.
- **Pacific Northwest.** Small car counts. Drivers use regional SLM series (e.g., Northwest Super Late Model Series, Evergreen Speedway) and Washington sprint cars (Kasey Kahne at Skagit/Deming), then move to Charlotte or go on tour. Greg Biffle was discovered at Tucson's Winter Heat. Most drivers from the region must **relocate** (assumed pattern, approx.).

## 14. Simulation Implications (design parameters)

All numbers are proposed calibration targets derived from the findings above, in 2025 USD. Multiply budgets by about 0.3–0.35 for a 1990s era (Dale Jr.'s LMSC comparison). Tune freely.

Enforce **track-type restrictions** by age: a 15-year-old ARCA driver is limited to short tracks and road courses, and a 16–17-year-old Truck driver to tracks ≤1.25 mi.

### 14.2 Licensing / approval mechanic
- A license object carries `approved_track_types = {short_oval_≤1mi, intermediate_≤1.5mi, large_oval, superspeedway, road_course, dirt}`.
- To unlock a track type, a driver needs: (a) the age threshold, (b) a résumé score (starts × quality-of-series weight; e.g., 20+ prior starts gives a high approval chance), and (c) for superspeedways, a test session meeting a speed threshold; for road courses, an observed test.
- An ARCA committee check with a lenient approval rate (about 85–95% for applicants with 5+ starts) can model the real-world criticism. Cup approval should be stricter (about 50–70% for outsiders), with a penalty for years inactive (the Mike Wallace case).

### 14.7 Funding-collapse events
- Each season at T5–T7, roll for **sponsor loss**: about 15–25%, scaling with the economy and the driver's marketability.
- On loss, choose a fallback, weighted by age and talent: part-time schedule (35%), drop a tier (25%), return to SLM/LMSC (20%), industry role such as engineer, spotter, coach or crew chief (15%), or retire (5%).
- **Industry-role drivers** keep a small (about 5%/yr) chance of a comeback ride (Majeski archetype).

### 14.8 Scouting / visibility model
- Each race has a **visibility score**:
  - weekly local = 1
  - regional tour = 3
  - ARCA or Truck = 5
  - crown jewels: Snowball Derby, Martinsville 300, Oxford 250, All American 400, Winchester 400, Slinger Nationals, Chili Bowl, Knoxville Nationals, Kings Royal, DLM Dream, World 100 = 8–10
- **Scouts** (manufacturer, Cup owner, Rev/NASCAR combine) sample 3–4 races per prospect per season and produce a noisy 1–10 rating adjusted for **equipment quality and field strength** (mirrors TRD).
- **Sim racing and social media** add a small marketability and visibility modifier. iRacing excellence can unlock an "IGNITE"-style combine invite for 13–16-year-olds.

### 14.9 Manufacturer AI profiles
- **Toyota:** systematic, analytics-heavy. Strong in dirt (KKM midgets). Supplies sponsors through B2B partners. Program size 8–12 stock car drivers plus 3 dirt drivers. Contracts are 1–3 years with release if the driver stalls. Some drivers defect when no Cup seat opens.
- **Chevrolet:** team-centric (Hendrick, JRM, Trackhouse). Fewer but bigger bets: personal development deals and long-term contracts.
- **Ford:** opportunistic. Signs proven ARCA/SLM winners and keeps drivers in non-driving roles.
- **NASCAR Driver Development (ex-D4D):** annual combine of 20 drivers aged 14–26; picks 6–10 and seats them at Rev Racing.

### 14.11 Veteran and retirement loop
- After Cup retirement, roll for a "**grassroots return**" with probability about 30–50%. The archetypes are Schrader, Wallace, Labonte, Kenseth and Harvick. The veteran runs 10–70 local or dirt races a year into their 60s–70s and may become a **series owner, track owner, team owner or agent**.
- These veterans can be NPC mentors who raise a young driver's skill growth rate, and NPC owners who offer late model seats (JRM, KHI/Rackley, KBM archetypes).

### 14.12 Dirt economics specifics
- A hired driver's income is `purse_share (0.30–0.40) × team_winnings`, plus a win bonus of about 5%.
- WoO sprint: about 90 nights a year. Top teams win $1M+. Points fund about $1.6M, tow money about $30–35k per full-time team.
- High Limit: about 50 nights, about $5M in purses. A charter gives 50% of streaming revenue in exchange for a must-race commitment.
- Crown-jewel winner payouts: $100–200k for dirt (Kings Royal $200k, Knoxville $195k, DLM Dream and World 100 $100k), $20–50k for asphalt (Snowball $50k, Martinsville about $32k + lap money, Oxford $25.1k, Slinger $20k + lap money, Winchester about $20–34.5k).
